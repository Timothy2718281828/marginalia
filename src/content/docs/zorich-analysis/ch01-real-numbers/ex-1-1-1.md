## Problem

Prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

## Analysis

This is the classic proof that irrational numbers exist. The core idea is **proof by contradiction**: assume $\sqrt{2}$ can be written as a reduced fraction $\frac{p}{q}$, then derive a contradiction using the uniqueness of prime factorization in $\Z$ (or a simple parity argument).

The key step: $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \implies p^2 = 2q^2$. The left-hand side has an even exponent for the prime $2$, the right-hand side has an odd exponent — contradiction.

## Solution

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{2} \in \Q$. Then there exist coprime positive integers $p, q$ such that

$$
\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}, \qquad \gcd(p, q) = 1.
$$

Squaring both sides:

$$
2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 2q^2. \tag{1}
$$

From (1), $p^2$ is even, hence $p$ is even. Write $p = 2k$ and substitute into (1):

$$
(2k)^2 = 2q^2 \implies 4k^2 = 2q^2 \implies q^2 = 2k^2.
$$

Thus $q^2$ is even, so $q$ is even. But this contradicts $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ — both $p$ and $q$ are divisible by $2$.

:::note[The unique factorization perspective]
More fundamentally, in equation (1), the exponent of the prime factor $2$ in $p^2$ is even (squaring doubles all exponents), while the exponent of $2$ in $2q^2$ is odd ($1$ + even = odd). This contradicts the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — the prime factorization of an integer is unique.
:::

Therefore the assumption is false: $\sqrt{2} \notin \Q$. $\square$

## Remarks

The proof relies essentially on the **unique factorization theorem** in $\Z$. The same method shows: if $n$ is not a perfect square, then $\sqrt{n}$ is irrational.

Exercise 1.1.2 generalizes this result to $\sqrt[k]{n}$.
